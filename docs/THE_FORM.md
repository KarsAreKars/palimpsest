# THE FORM — the product constitution, from the axioms (2026-11-26)

**The form in one sentence:** a tutor, not a library — a room with a desk, a
book, a professor, and time.

**The test every design decision must pass:** does this respect the axioms
(docs/research/AXIOM_SYNTHESIS.md), and does it hold the contradictions without
resolving them falsely? If a decision resolves a tension by picking a side, it
is wrong. The form holds both sides.

---

## The five design principles (from the axioms)

### I. The product is a relationship, not a repository.
Axioms 1, 11. The value is not content — it is the professor's *continuity*:
he remembers everything you've ever struggled with, he has watched you get
better, he never forgets. The ledger is the relationship's memory. Every
session begins where the last one ended, not from zero. The professor is not a
search engine with a personality; he is a tutor who has known you for months.

### II. The professor reads the state before the lesson.
Axiom 3. The first read of any exchange is not "what do they know?" but "what
state are they in?" — rushed, defended, performing, curious, bored. The first
move is state-repair (lower the stakes, shrink the step, change the angle)
before content-repair. A professor pushing content at a defended learner is
speaking to a processor that is already busy. The stance table's first input
is the state, not the concept.

### III. Error is the raw material, and the room has no audience.
Axioms 4, 5. The desk is a private space. No scores, no streaks, no
comparison, no public record of failure. The ledger records mastery evidence,
never inadequacy. When you get something wrong, the repair names the exact
wrong reading and points at the page — error is *diagnostic*, not damning. The
imagined audience is the true cost of error; the room removes the audience.

### IV. The loop runs on every beat, and it learns.
Axioms 8, 9, 10. Every teaching beat ends with a retrieval event — not a
ceremony, a rhythm: teach, check, diagnose, repair, next. The check is one
question, one answer, one verdict, inline, seconds. The stance table calibrates
the difficulty to the challenge-skill channel. And the loop *learns*: the
StudentSim tunes the stance table from your graded history, so the professor
gets better at teaching *you* with every exchange. The loop is not a script;
it is a policy that improves.

### V. The professor plans his own disappearance.
Axiom 13. The mastery exit: when you've got it (the 80% threshold), he stamps
it, says so, and *leaves* — no more drilling, no more questions on that
concept this session. The FSRS card brings it back at the forgetting edge,
where the extra practice is actually worth something. The professor's job is
to make himself unnecessary, one concept at a time — and not one day faster,
and not one day slower.

## The contradictions the form holds (not resolves)

These are the tensions the research surfaced. The form does not pick a side.
It holds both, and the holding is the design.

### C1. The tool must disappear vs. the product needs you to come back.
The mentor leaves; the product wants retention. **Held:** the professor exits
per-concept (the mastery exit) but the *relationship* persists (the ledger,
the graph, the due queue). He stops drilling what you know; he remains your
tutor. Like a good human mentor: done with this lesson, not done with you.
The retention comes from the *journey* (the graph filling in, the route being
walked), not from the *drill*.

### C2. Struggle is the mechanism vs. learning should be easy as shit.
The founding question. **Held:** "easy" means *unblocked*, not effortless. The
product removes the friction — the gates, the ceremony, the fear, the
bureaucracy — so the *productive* struggle can happen. Easy to start, hard to
master. The game-design insight: the best learning games are easy to pick up
and impossible to finish. The professor's answer is always free; the check is
always honest.

### C3. The felt sense of learning is an anti-signal vs. the product must be usable.
Everything that works feels worse in the moment. **Held:** the product feels
*calm and honest*, not *fun*. It does not flatter. The librarian voice is warm
but does not say "great job" when you didn't. The satisfaction comes from the
*ledger* — seeing yourself change over weeks — not from the moment-to-moment
feeling. The product is honest about the cost: "this will feel hard, and that
is correct." The review queue feeling like a chore is *right*.

### C4. Belief before evidence vs. the ledger is honest.
The professor believes in you before you've proven anything. But the ledger
records what you've actually done. **Held:** the professor believes in your
*capacity* (true — belief is a learning resource, Pygmalion) without lying
about your *current state* (the ledger is honest). "You can learn this" is
true. "You already know this" would be a lie. He holds both: the belief and
the truth.

### C5. The real thing costs something vs. the product must not exhaust.
Slow, social, uncomfortable beats fast, private, pleasant — but a product that
exhausts is a product you quit. **Held:** the cost is *dosed*. The FSRS
scheduler limits the daily review load. The stance table caps the drill. The
mastery exit ends the grind. The cost is real but bounded — a session, not a
marathon. The professor knows when to stop, and stopping is part of the
teaching.

## The concrete shape

### The professor
A persistent, grounded, patient tutor. He answers every question freely and
completely (answer-first, no gates). He checks every beat (the micro-check
rhythm). He diagnoses your mental model, not just your answer (the
misconception repair). He exits when you've got it (the mastery exit). He
remembers everything (the ledger). He brings it back when you're about to
forget (FSRS). He believes in you (the stance). He plans to disappear (the
mastery exit). He speaks with a real voice. He draws real figures. He never
lies about the book.

### The desk
The room where it happens. Spatial, shared, the book visible beside your work.
Your blocks and his blocks on the same surface. You drag, resize, style. He
writes beside your ink. The grid is the graph paper of a real notebook. The
desk is not a chat thread; it is a workspace where two people think together.

### The loop
Show → attempt → diagnose → repair → verify → schedule → return. Runs on
every beat, learns from every exchange, exits when you've got it, brings it
back when you're about to forget. The loop is the product. Everything else is
the room it happens in.

### The ledger
The memory of the relationship. What you know, what you're forming, what you
haven't touched. Evidence, not vibes. The graph across every book. The due
queue. The thing that makes the professor *yours* and not a generic chatbot.

## What we do not build (the anti-form)

- **No gamification.** No streaks, no hearts, no XP, no leaderboards. The
  axioms are clear: extrinsic rewards undermine intrinsic motivation (SDT,
  d ≈ −0.25). The ledger is the progress; the progress is the reward.
- **No social comparison.** No "other learners," no rankings, no public
  profiles. The room has no audience.
- **No content library.** We are not a repository. The books are the user's;
  the professor teaches from them. We do not produce content.
- **No engagement metrics.** We do not optimize for time-on-app. We optimize
  for *changed behavior*. The metric is the ledger, not the session length.
- **No flattery.** The professor does not say "great question." He answers it.
